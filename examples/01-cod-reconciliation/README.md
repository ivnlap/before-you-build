# Example 01 — Cash-on-delivery reconciliation → CHANGE

> Illustrative example. People and numbers are invented.

**The idea.** Small online shops that sell through Instagram and messengers in Central Asian cities often ship with third-party couriers who collect cash on delivery. Reconciling who paid, which courier holds which cash, and what the courier service owes takes hours. Idea: a simple tool that matches orders, deliveries and cash payouts automatically.

**What happened in two weeks.**

1. `byb-assumptions` put **"shops pay $15/month for this"** (A4) as the riskiest fatal bet — the problem looked real, the willingness to pay didn't. It also *removed* a feasibility bet: instead of integrating with courier services' systems, the first version would work from the spreadsheets shops already keep.
2. **Experiment 01 — problem interviews** (8 shops). Time cost confirmed at L2: 6 of 8 showed a weekly spreadsheet. But only 1 of 8 could name a loss caused by it — between the lines, so the verdict was **CHANGE** the framing from "lost money" to "lost time". Unplanned signal: 3 courier-agency owners, reached through the shops, described disputes that cost real money.
3. **Experiment 02 — pre-sale to shops** with a refundable $15 deposit: 1 of 15 qualified shops paid — the kill line was ≤ 2.
4. `byb-decide`: **CHANGE** — keep the problem, switch the paying segment to small courier agencies. Next: experiment 03, a paid pilot offer to 6 agencies.

**Look at:** how the decision memo separates what the data says from what it doesn't ([decisions.md](validation/cod-reconciliation/decisions.md)), and how the unplanned interview signal becomes one specific change rather than a vague "pivot".
