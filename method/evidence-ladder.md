# The evidence ladder — field guide

Every observation you log gets a level. The level decides how much a piece of evidence is allowed to change your mind.

## L0 — Opinion about the future
**Sounds like:** "I'd use that." "Sounds amazing." "You should build it." "Let me know when it launches."
**Why it's weak:** People are polite, want to be helpful, and are poor predictors of their own future behaviour. A compliment costs them nothing.
**Use it for:** Nothing, except as a prompt to ask a better question ("When did you last run into this?").
**Watch out:** A pile of L0 is still L0. Fifty friends saying "great idea" is not evidence.

## L1 — Stated past behaviour
**Sounds like:** "Last Tuesday I spent two hours fixing the courier schedule by hand." "We tried two tools last year and dropped both."
**Why it matters:** The past already happened; it's much harder to be optimistic about it.
**Strength test:** Is it *specific* (a date, a number, a name of a tool)? Generic statements ("I usually…", "we always…") are closer to L0.

## L2 — Observed behaviour or artifact
**Looks like:** You saw the workaround spreadsheet. Competitor reviews complaining about the same gap. Search volume. Forum threads. Job posts paying people to do the task manually. Existing spend on an alternative.
**Why it matters:** It exists independently of your conversation.
**Note:** All desk research lives here at best and is tagged `desk`. It tells you a problem is real for *someone*; it doesn't tell you *your* target will choose *your* solution.

## L3 — Low-cost action
**Looks like:** Clicked "Buy now" on a fake door. Joined a waitlist with a real email. Replied to a cold message. Booked a call. Downloaded a lead magnet.
**Why it matters:** They did something, not said something. Cheap actions still filter out pure politeness.
**Strength test:** Did the action cost them anything — time, an email, a click past a price?

## L4 — Commitment
**Looks like:** Spent 30+ minutes on a call or a session. Shared real data or files. Introduced you to their boss or the person who pays. Used a manual/concierge version more than once. Agreed to a pilot start date.
**Why it matters:** They spent something scarce — time, reputation, effort.

## L5 — Money
**Looks like:** Pre-payment. Deposit. Paid pilot. Signed letter of intent *with a price*. Paid for the concierge version.
**Why it matters:** The cleanest signal there is. Even a small amount separates "nice to have" from "worth paying for".

---

## Rules of thumb

- **Required level grows with the bet.** Spending a weekend? L2 may be enough. Quitting a job or hiring? Get to L4–L5 first.
- **Count and show the denominator.** "3 of 12 cold emails got a reply", not "people replied".
- **One source never carries a segment.** A single interview or a single customer is an anecdote, not a pattern.
- **Contradicting evidence counts double in your attention.** Log it first, explain it never.
- **Mixed levels don't average.** Ten L0s and one L5 is "one L5 and some noise", not "medium".

## Small numbers, honestly

Early tests have small samples. That's fine — just be honest about what they can say:

- Fewer than ~5 data points: anecdote. Useful for direction, never for a verdict on its own.
- 5–20: directional. Enough to STOP if the result is very bad, or to CONTINUE to a stronger test if it's very good. Not enough to fine-tune a price.
- Set thresholds as **counts** at this scale ("at least 3 of 10 pre-pay"), not as percentages with false precision.
- When your threshold is driven by economics ("we need 5% landing conversion for ads to pay back"), say so — it's the best kind of threshold.
